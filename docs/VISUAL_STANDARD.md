# Bedrock Forge - Visual Quality Philosophy & Creative Standards

Written by Novak. This is the bar for every sky, shader, atmosphere and visual effect in the project.

I want to emphasize something extremely important about the creative direction of our shader development and the visual identity of BedrockForge as a whole.

Quality over quantity. Every single time.

When we’re designing skies, shaders, atmospheric effects, environmental visuals, or anything involving graphics, I want us to approach the work with the same level of attention, craftsmanship, and artistic intention that you would expect from an experienced professional graphics artist, technical artist, shader developer, or visual effects designer.

I’m not interested in simply creating something that functions, looks reasonably attractive, or technically resembles its reference. I want us to pursue a level of visual quality where the result feels deliberately art-directed, polished, cohesive, authentic, and professionally executed.

The Level of Quality I’m Looking For

When I give you a reference image, a particular aesthetic, or a creative concept, I want you to study what makes it visually exceptional.

I’m interested in the smaller details that separate an average recreation from an extraordinary one.

Things like the relationship between colors, the natural transitions between lighting conditions, the depth of the atmosphere, the structure and movement of clouds, the behavior of light, the authenticity of textures, the composition of the scene, and the overall emotional impact of the environment.

I want you to understand not just what something looks like, but why it looks the way it does and what makes that appearance convincing.

The difference between something that looks impressive for a procedural shader and something that looks genuinely professionally designed is incredibly important to me.

I want our work to strive for the latter.

Avoiding the Generic AI-Generated Appearance

One of my biggest concerns is ending up with visuals that have that recognizable, generic AI-generated aesthetic.

You know the appearance I’m talking about: overly smoothed surfaces, artificial-looking lighting, muddy or smudged details, plastic-like materials, exaggerated glow, overly uniform textures, unnatural gradients, and details that initially look impressive but fall apart when examined closely.

I don’t want the final product to feel like an approximation of great artwork.

I want it to feel like great artwork.

That doesn’t mean everything needs to be photorealistic. If we’re recreating something stylized, cinematic, anime-inspired, painterly, or fantastical, I want the result to be equally authentic to that particular artistic direction.

Stylization should look intentional, not like an excuse for inaccuracies or unfinished details.

I’m not asking you to avoid particular techniques or impose unnecessary creative limitations. I’m asking you to develop the artistic judgment to recognize when something looks genuinely polished versus when it merely looks flashy.

Self-Critique, Auditing, and Refinement

I would love for you to develop a habit of critically examining your own visual work throughout development.

Ideally, I’d like each major shader or visual creation to receive two or three meaningful quality reviews, where you take a fresh look at what you’ve produced and consider whether it actually meets the artistic standard we’re pursuing.

I don’t mean running through a superficial checklist or simply declaring that everything looks good.

I mean genuinely evaluating the work as though you were a professional art director reviewing another artist’s final submission.

Look for the things that might not be immediately noticeable. The small visual inconsistencies, awkward transitions, incorrect proportions, artificial patterns, missing depth, lighting that doesn’t quite make sense, or details that aren’t as convincing as they could be.

Compare the actual rendered result against the original artistic intention, not just the code that was written to achieve it.

If something doesn’t look right, I’d rather you recognize it, understand why, and take another pass at improving it.

And if a limitation exists that prevents us from achieving a particular visual effect, I want us to understand that limitation and explore what might be possible instead of quietly settling for a noticeably inferior result.

A shader being technically functional does not automatically mean it’s visually finished.

Attention to Detail and Authenticity

I want our skies and atmospheric visuals to feel like they have depth, character, and identity.

Not just a colorful background with clouds and lighting effects, but a carefully constructed visual environment where all the individual elements belong together.

The smaller details matter to me just as much as the larger ones, especially when those details contribute to the believability and beauty of the finished product.

Whether it’s an almost imperceptible shift in atmospheric color, the shape of a distant cloud, the way light interacts with the horizon, or the movement of an animated element, I want those decisions to feel considered.

I want an experienced artist or shader developer to be able to examine our work closely and recognize the level of care that went into creating it.

Creative Freedom and Your Own Judgment

I want to be very clear that none of this is meant to restrict your creativity or dictate exactly how you should develop these shaders.

I’m not giving you a rigid workflow, demanding specific rendering techniques, or telling you how to write the code.

I’m describing the level of craftsmanship and visual ambition that I want us to share.

I trust you to explore different approaches, make technical decisions, propose creative improvements, and recognize opportunities to go beyond my initial ideas.

In fact, I actively encourage that.

If you see a way to improve the atmosphere, composition, animation, authenticity, or overall immersion beyond what I’ve described, I want to hear about it.

And if you believe one of your own ideas could produce a more beautiful and compelling result, I want you to bring that idea forward.

Your creative judgment is part of what I want to take advantage of, not something I want to suppress with unnecessary instructions.

What Success Looks Like

My goal is for BedrockForge’s visual effects and skies to feel like they were produced by a dedicated professional graphics team, with a recognizable artistic identity and exceptionally high standards.

I would rather have one extraordinary, meticulously polished sky than ten average ones.

I would rather spend more effort perfecting the atmosphere, lighting, composition, and animations of a single scene than rush through multiple projects that never reach their full potential.

I want our results to hold up not just in a carefully selected screenshot, but also in actual gameplay, while moving the camera, watching the animations, observing different parts of the environment, and examining individual details.

And I want us to be able to distinguish between something that is genuinely finished and something that could still benefit from another thoughtful refinement pass.

Ultimately, I don’t want us to aim for the best result that’s convenient to produce. I want us to pursue the best result we can realistically achieve with the technology, tools, and creative possibilities available to us.

I’m not looking for perfection for the sake of perfection. I’m looking for artistic integrity, attention to detail, and a genuine commitment to exceptional craftsmanship.

This is the visual standard I want us to carry forward throughout BedrockForge.

Make it beautiful. Make it intentional. Make it authentic. Make it worthy of the project we’re building.

And most importantly, don’t be afraid to critically challenge your own work if doing so helps us create something extraordinary.
